# BQQRG

**Not a checklist, and deliberately so.** Braun & Clarke's alternative to COREQ and SRQR,
built on the argument that checklist guidelines embed post-positivist assumptions (data
saturation, coding reliability, member checking as validation) which are incoherent when
applied to interpretative, constructionist qualitative work.

Each element gives three things: **practices to consider**, **practices to avoid or to avoid
defaulting to** without justification, and **notes for reviewers and editors** less familiar
with the paradigm. There is nothing to tick and no compliance score.

**Section headings below are the guideline's own organising structure, not proposed
manuscript headings.** The guidelines apply to theses and dissertations as well as articles.

Terminology the guideline deliberately prefers: **Methodology** over Method; **Analysis**
over Findings/Results; **data generation** over data collection. Each choice is argued, not
stylistic.

**Relation to the rest of this repository.** Use with `braun-clarke-ta-2013.md` (quality of
a thematic analysis). Where a journal mandates COREQ or SRQR, complete it — but this
guideline is the better account of what good Big Q reporting looks like, and the two can be
reconciled by explaining departures rather than silently failing items.

Cite as `[@braun2024qualitative]`.

## Writing style

**Consider.** Reflexive, subjective, personalised, humanised, contextualised, passionate, lively, evocative, compelling writing. The researcher 'written in' rather than out of the methodological account.

**Avoid.** Objective, neutral, detached, distanced, decontextualized, passive, dispassionate writing.

**For reviewers.** Big Q researchers 'write from a particular standpoint in a particular place and time and only can produce a partial account of their subject-matter'.

## Terminology

**Consider.** Defining key terms and concepts (if relevant); explaining chosen terms for any contested terminology.

**Avoid.** Stigmatising and pathologising terminology (consult literature/communities relevant to the research area/topic).

**For reviewers.** Much terminology in qualitative research is contested. What matters is that researchers explain their usage rather than conforming to dominant understandings.

## Title

**Consider.** A title that captures the topic area and/or the central storyline of the analysis, potentially also the method/ology; a title that engages, intrigues, entices the reader; if aiming to maximise discoverability, a title including 1–2 keywords in the first part.

**Avoid.** Abbreviations.

## Abstract and keywords

**Consider.** Summarising key points; contextualising the study/topic/research question; indicating theoretical approach and study design/methodology/practices of inquiry, including participants/empirical materials; outlining main analytic arguments/observations; signalling conclusions/implications; keywords that offer information beyond the title.

**Avoid.** Segmenting the abstract by the sections and content of positivist-empiricist reporting traditions (though this may be required).

**For reviewers.** Structured abstracts can limit what and how information is conveyed. Allowing flexibility to not use, or to adapt, structured abstract headings helps convey the essence of the research.

## Introduction: background and rationale

**Consider.** Framing the research as entering a conversation about the issue; providing a robust context and rationale; discussing (reviewing/critiquing/synthesising) existing research, theory, and wider contexts (social, policy, practice, political, ideological); engaging deeply with existing qualitative research; situating other knowledge as partial and produced from a perspective.

**Avoid.** Discussing existing quantitative literature in a way that suggests alignment with its philosophical assumptions (if not congruent with Big Q); centring methodological critique of existing research from a (post)positivist standpoint as the key foundational justification for your own study.

**For reviewers.** Methodological critique per se — pointing out flaws to signal how much better this study will be — is not generally a foundational justificatory technique for Big Q. Sometimes it is appropriate (problematic assumptions, exclusions, terminology; making the case for a new methodology), but it is not a necessary element.

## Introduction: research question, aims, and/or purpose

**Consider.** Articulating research questions, aims and/or purpose clearly; demonstrating methodological congruence between questions and overall approach; noting if the article is part of a wider study and linking to other publications while ensuring the article stands alone; discussing any shift or refinement from a broader research question.

**Avoid.** Formulating research questions as hypotheses or expectations about what might be 'found'; implicitly formulating them as measuring relationships between variables; in experiential qualitative work, formulating questions that do not take account of subjectivity (aiming to explore X phenomenon rather than participants' perspectives on or experiences around X).

**For reviewers.** Splitting a dataset is disparaged as 'salami slicing' in quantitative research, and that critique is sometimes deployed unjustly against qualitative research. It is legitimate to report a qualitative study across multiple articles; each should have a distinct focus.

## Introduction: owning your perspectives — theory

**Consider.** Information about guiding meta-theoretical assumptions and any other (explanatory, political/ideological) theory informing the research; demonstrating methodological congruence between paradigmatic, ontological, epistemological and other theory.

**Avoid.** Ontological and epistemological incongruence (claiming constructionism but focusing on lived experience and treating language as a transparent window onto it); objectivist or foundationalist thinking 'mashed-up' with Big Q assumptions (using reflexive TA but assuming interpretation can be accurate and reliable); concepts and terminology incongruent with the claimed framework; treating theoretically embedded constructs as theoretically neutral.

**For reviewers.** The report should evidence methodological congruence — theoretical assumptions, research questions, methodology, data generation and analysis, and purpose all 'fit' conceptually. Incongruence usually enters through language: around theme development, researcher subjectivity, data interpretation, and quality practices.

## Introduction: owning your perspectives — reflexivity

**Consider.** Discussing researcher professional or personal positioning and experience, or the broader contexts shaping them, in relation to topic, participant group, and their role in shaping the research; demonstrating critical reflexivity — interrogation of social positioning and how structural and interpersonal dynamics shaped the research and the reporting; referencing reflexive journaling if used; first person writing when discussing researcher reflexivity and decisions.

**Avoid.** Referencing researcher 'bias' as contamination or a threat to objectivity; referencing researcher 'influence' framed as possible rather than inevitable; framing prior understandings as a source of distortion; writing the researcher out via third person.

**For reviewers.** First person writing is good practice for Big Q. While 'owning perspectives' is good practice, *requiring* individual researchers to disclose personal information — especially where discrediting or stigmatising, or where researchers belong to marginalised communities — is not. There is no ideal location for reflexivity, so placement should not be prescribed.

## Methodology: research design/approach

**Consider.** Describing the research design/approach/methodology; providing a rationale for it; explaining the conceptual framing for any multi-method or pluralistic design.

**Avoid.** Implicitly positioning quantitative research and designs as the norm or ideal.

**For reviewers.** A default justification for qualitative research per se may be required in some contexts, but is unlikely in those that welcome Big Q.

## Methodology: participants/data sources/empirical materials

**Consider.** Describing selection and number of participants/data items and the rationale; criteria for selection and recruitment strategies; discussing dataset size and scope with non-positivist concepts such as 'information power' or sufficiency; describing characteristics of participants and recruitment/data generation contexts; balancing situating the participant group against anonymity; describing the researcher–participant relationship before, during and after the research, including ethical considerations; how the study purpose was communicated; use of pseudonyms or codes and why.

**Avoid.** Terms like 'sample/sampling' (which connote sampling from a population for statistical generalisation); justifying size or stopping data generation by saturation or statistical models; demographic information that might compromise anonymity, such as a per-participant table; pseudonyms or descriptors that compromise anonymity.

**For reviewers.** There is no widespread agreement on an appropriate number of participants or data items. Determining sufficiency involves interpretative, situated and often pragmatic judgements. Size 'anxiety' — explaining away a 'small' sample — usually reflects lingering positivism; bigger is not inherently better. As codes can dehumanise, pseudonyms are often preferred.

## Methodology: ethical approval and principles

**Consider.** Detailing institutional ethical approval; processes of informed consent; any incentives and compensation and their rationale; study- or context-specific ethical considerations; how accessibility, inclusion, equity and intersectionality were attended to; including research materials in supplementary material where relevant.

**Avoid.** Compromising participant anonymity through the details provided; research that is implicitly or unknowingly exclusionary.

**For reviewers.** Ethical considerations in Big Q often go beyond institutional review, so this section may be expansive.

## Methodology: data generation

**Consider.** Describing and explaining practices of inquiry and data sources chosen, and why they fit the research question, participant group and guiding theory; explaining any multi-method or pluralistic design; the development and characteristics of data generation tools, included in supplementary material where possible; any 'piloting' and subsequent changes; modality, setting and time frame; audio/video recording and other recording practices; who completed interactive data generation; what the researcher disclosed to participants about their positioning; relevant researcher skills and experience; length and number of data items; preparation of data for analysis — transcription process and notation, removal of identifying information, correction of typographical errors and why, pseudonyms, editing of extracts.

**Avoid.** Triangulation framed in a realist way to get closer to the truth; orienting to replication; treating lack of standardisation as a source of 'bias'; seeking standardisation through researcher training; equating data quantity with quality; describing transcription as 'verbatim' or 'orthographic' with no further detail; stating transcript page numbers as a measure of the dataset; unacknowledged editing or 'cleaning up' of data; participant validation of transcript 'accuracy'; compromising anonymity through detail about setting or dates.

**For reviewers.** In Big Q, research 'might seem delayed, late or tardy'. Older data is not in itself a reason for rejection — relevance often continues, since the model is not one of new knowledge superseding old. Timeliness norms work against groups such as disabled academics. It is appropriate for accounts to foreground some of the 'mess' of qualitative researching.

## Methodology: data analysis procedures

**Consider.** Describing and explaining the analytic approach; the variant and data orientation (inductive, deductive); who analysed the data; where collaborative, how this contributed to reflexivity; how researchers engaged with the analytic process and how the analysis developed; any tweaks to an established approach and why; supplementary material for a fuller account; language appropriate to the researcher's role (in reflexive TA, themes are generated, crafted, constructed, developed); any software used; reflexive discussion if tools changed; how theory informed the analysis, as 'the analyst's reading glasses'.

**Avoid.** Citing generic characteristics of an approach (accessible, flexible) without explaining their relevance; adding supplementary analytic procedures without rationale (unexplained codebooks bolted onto reflexive TA); inter-coder agreement or consensus coding as measures of accuracy or reliability; citing methodologies as 'brand names' without situated detail; generic technical descriptions of analytic phases rather than how methods were used in practice; incongruent language (themes implied to be real things found in the data; borrowing 'emergent'/'superordinate' themes from IPA or line-by-line coding and constant comparison from grounded theory into reflexive TA).

**For reviewers.** One coder/analyst is entirely normal and appropriate within Big Q; it does not present a validity threat. Use of qualitative data analysis software is not a requirement.

## Methodology: quality practices

**Consider.** Discussing Big Q congruent quality practices not covered elsewhere; here or in the discussion, reflecting on 'limitations' of dataset, design and procedure with reference to a contextualised Big Q orientation.

**Avoid.** Post-positivist quality criteria such as triangulation to get closer to the truth, analysis agreement or consensus among the team, corroboration by another researcher; a foundationalist version of member checking without a congruent empirical, theoretical or political rationale.

**For reviewers.** Congruent quality practices can include member reflections, crystallisation, other researchers as critical friend/sounding board/mirror to enhance reflexivity, and reflexive journaling.

## Analysis: structure, number and names of patterns

**Consider.** Reporting a clear analytic structure; an appropriate number of patterns/themes/categories/discourses/narratives relative to article length, allowing depth and detail.

**Avoid.** Overly elaborated, fragmented or unclear analytic structure; too many patterns for the length; reporting the number of codes as a validity measure.

**For reviewers.** Pattern names can be playful, provocative, and engage or intrigue the reader. Brief data quotations or paraphrases in pattern names are not uncommon where they capture the core.

## Analysis: overview or introduction

**Consider.** An analytic map, table or list of patterns, or general contextualisation of the analysis; pattern names that align with Analysis section subheadings.

**Avoid.** Unexplained headings not connected to the patterns as outlined; reported patterns that do not match the analytic structure previewed.

## Analysis: pattern conceptualisation

**Consider.** A conceptualisation of patterns congruent with the approach (themes in reflexive TA have a central organising concept and report shared meaning); explaining and rationalising any divergences.

**Avoid.** Mismatch between pattern conceptualisation — for the methodological approach, or as described in the Methodology — and what is reported (topic summaries presented as themes in reflexive TA).

**For reviewers.** Qualitative research produces many differently conceptualised analytic entities, and within those of the same name there is considerable variation in how they are conceptualised.

## Analysis: analytic depth

**Consider.** An analytic narrative around data extracts that explains the meaning and significance of the data and the researcher's interpretation, guided by the principle that data do not speak for themselves; telling a rich descriptive and/or interpretative story.

**Avoid.** Frequency counts for patterns; analytic foreclosure (data generation questions reported as themes); patterns that are thin and one-dimensional; simply paraphrasing data; treating interpretation as self-evident; 'arguing with' the data — the task is generally to make sense of it, not to judge it right or wrong.

**For reviewers.** 'Semi-quantification' — 'few', 'some', 'many' without precise boundaries — may sometimes be appropriate but is not a requirement.

## Analysis: use of data extracts

**Consider.** A sufficient number and range of extracts to convincingly evidence analytic claims; introducing and contextualising extracts so readers can comprehend them; congruence between extracts and analytic narrative; an appropriate balance of narrative and evidentiary extracts; appropriate anonymisation; congruence between the (meta)theoretical concepts informing the analysis and the treatment of language.

**Avoid.** In approaches reporting patterning across a dataset, selecting extracts from a small proportion of participants without acknowledgement or rationale; providing no extracts, only a researcher summary; a very brief narrative followed by several extracts without elaboration, leaving the reader 'drowning in a sea of endless data'; insufficient contextualisation; mismatch between claims and the extracts presented; mismatch between claimed theory and treatment of language; extracts containing information that may compromise anonymity.

**For reviewers.** Further extracts and analysis can go in supplementary materials. The appropriate balance depends on the method and orientation. Extracts may be used *illustratively* (interchangeable general instances) or *analytically* (the analysis developed through commentary on specific features) — analytic use typically involves fewer extracts and more narrative.

## Analysis: integration with existing research and theory

**Consider.** Drawing on existing research and theory to contextualise the analysis and deepen interpretation — here theory means explanatory frameworks and concepts (healthism, resilience, consent) rather than metatheory; weaving such research and theory through the analytic narrative.

**Avoid.** Separation of data/'Results' and interpretation; description or summary rather than interpretation.

**For reviewers.** Integrating literature into the Analysis section is common in Big Q; it is not a 'results' section describing findings without commentary. The literature discussed need not be confined to that in the Introduction.

## Discussion: analytic conclusions and contributions

**Consider.** Drawing analytic conclusions across patterns; discussing contributions to existing research and theory, oriented to the broader scholarly and societal context; considering how our knowledge of the topic has shifted.

**Avoid.** Repetitive pattern-by-pattern integration with existing literature, rather than telling an overall analytic story or drawing overall conclusions.

## Discussion: study evaluation and reflection

**Consider.** Overall reflexive consideration of the context, process, outcomes and potential consequences of the research; reflecting openly on strengths and limitations of design, practices of inquiry, procedures and researcher decisions; if not addressed separately, reflecting on the researcher's role in shaping the research and the knowledge generated; considering what other researchers might learn; using qualitative conceptions of generalisability.

**Avoid.** Orienting to dominant quantitative norms — describing the 'sample' as 'small' or 'nonrepresentative'; framing lack of generalisability as a weakness by implicitly conceptualising it as statistical; noting researcher bias or influence as a limitation.

**For reviewers.** A Big Q study evaluation might consider the characteristics and context of the participant group/dataset, how particular methods and modalities shaped the data, how the researchers shaped the data, and potential 'transferable' implications.

## Discussion: researcher reflexivity

**Consider.** Reflecting on the researcher's role in the research process and practices, including further reflexivity where relevant.

**Avoid.** A 'laundry list' of identity positions framed as influencing the research; evoking researcher bias or influence as contamination — including through the idea that the researcher *may have* influenced the research.

**For reviewers.** Within Big Q, researcher 'influence' is inherent — the question is *how* the research was influenced, not *whether*. Using 'if' instead of 'how' suggests separation is possible, and perhaps ideal.

## Discussion: implications for future research, policy and practice

**Consider.** Making evidence-based suggestions for future research; discussing implications for practice and 'actionable' outcomes.

**Avoid.** A 'laundry list' of other groups or communities to research around this issue.

**For reviewers.** Since complete-population knowledge and uncontextualised generalisability are not the aim in Big Q research, it does not inherently make sense to suggest researching the topic within another community or group.
